# From Kripke's formula and the Benjamin Franklin passage.

The necessity of identity formula ∀x∀y(x=y → $□$(x=y)) mentioned in "Identity and Necessity" [@munitzIdentityIndividuation1971, p.136 ] opening argument, is valid because variables under assignment are pure extensional pointers. When an assignment maps x and y to the same domain element d, that assignment holds fixed across possible worlds. The variables point at d and stay pointing at d — no descriptive content, no intensional profile, nothing to re-evaluate. A variable under assignment functions exactly like a demonstrative: "this one here," nothing more. The formula's rigidity is demonstrative rigidity in formal dress.

Now look at how Kripke applies the formula in "Identity and Necessity." Two descriptions pick out an individual each ("invented bifocals," "was first Postmaster General") — contingently, since a different person could have done either thing. Then Kripke asks "What are x and y here?" and answers: "x and y are both Benjamin Franklin, and it can certainly be necessary that Benjamin Franklin is identical with himself." Two slides happen in this sentence, each compounding the other.

The first is a slide from mathematical object to concrete object. "Object" in "there is an object x such that..." must mean domain element — a mathematical point, self-identical by construction. If it meant a concrete particular, Wittgenstein's dilemma would apply immediately: saying two things are identical is nonsensical, saying one thing is self-identical says nothing (Tractatus 5.5303). But "object x" also evokes something encountered, a man one might meet and name. The word bridges the mathematical and the concrete without marking the transition.

The second is a slide from variables as placeholders to assigned variables. In the formula, x and y are placeholders — they range over a domain under a universal quantifier. That is what makes the formula general: _for every_ x and y. But "x and y are both Benjamin Franklin" treats x and y as pinned to one particular value. And "are" goes further: it collapses the distance between the variable and its value, as if x and y _became_ the man rather than _pointing to_ him. The variables stop doing variable-work. The two independent descriptive paths that made the identity informative disappear into a single name, and what remains is trivial self-identity.

The two slides compound. The first makes the formula look like it is about real things rather than mathematical elements. The second makes the variables look like they were the person all along rather than formal devices that happened to receive the same assignment. Together they produce the impression that the formula establishes something substantive about Benjamin Franklin, when what it establishes is that demonstrative-type pointing — bare extensional assignment — is rigid. That is local rigidity. The question the formula cannot answer is how names achieve the same result without an assignment function or perceptual contact. PRU answers it through baptismal lifting: the operation that takes $x^o$-mode tracking (local, demonstrative, perceptual) and stabilises it into $N^c$ (a socially portable device that works across absence).

# From Russell

Russell proposed that demonstratives are the only "real proper names"—words that "just name an object without describing it" (quoted by Kripke in [@munitzIdentityIndividuation1971, p. 142] and explicit in [@russellLogicKnowledgeEssays p. 198] ). He recognized that referential certainty—necessity of identity between names, immune from doubt—holds only for demonstratives of immediate sense data.

In PRU terms, Russell discovered $x^o$. A demonstrative coordinates with a particular without descriptive mediation. Identity at the demonstrative level is either trivially true (one object, pointed at twice) or nonsensical (two objects, cannot be identical). No epistemic gap.

Russell's retreat: he demanded Cartesian infallibility, so he restricted genuine naming to immediate sense data. Everything else became disguised description. PRU drops the infallibility requirement. $x^o$ is fallible—you can misrecognize—but the mechanism is the same: direct coordination with a particular, not satisfaction of a description. Local rigidity generalizes once certainty is no longer required.

# From the modal failure of descriptions

A description like "the inventor of bifocals" operates entirely within $⟨x⟩$ — it connects words to words: "inventor," "bifocals," a uniqueness condition. It is $x^c$ through and through. There is no particular being coordinated with, no demonstrative contact, no "this one here." The phrase has no $x^o$ dimension at all.

In a modal context, this matters. Because the description has no $x^o$ anchor, there is nothing to hold fixed across possible worlds. "The inventor of bifocals" connects to whoever sits at the right node of the $⟨x⟩$ network in each world independently — Franklin in this world, perhaps someone else in another. The description does not track an individual. It locates a position in linguistic space, and that position can be occupied differently in different worlds.

This is not a defect of particular descriptions. It is what descriptions are. A description is a structure within $⟨x⟩$, and $⟨x⟩$ structures are world-relative by nature. No amount of enriching the description changes this. You still have a linguistic network being navigated, not a particular being held.

When you take the description outside $□$ (Russell's primary occurrence, large scope), rigidity seems to appear: fix who actually satisfies the description, then hold that person fixed across worlds. But look at what happened. The description did the locating — it navigated $⟨x⟩$ and found someone. The holding is a separate act. You identified a particular and are now keeping your grip on it through counterfactual variation. That grip is $x^o$: demonstrative tracking, prior to and independent of the description. The description contributed the $⟨x⟩$ navigation; something else contributed the hold.

Kaplan's dthat(the inventor of bifocals) makes the two steps visible as two steps. First, evaluate the description in the actual world — navigate $⟨x⟩$, locate a particular ($x^c$). Then freeze the result as a rigid term — switch to $x^o$ mode, hold that particular. The operator does not extract rigidity from the description. It imports $x^o$ from outside.

So descriptions can simulate rigidity only by borrowing from the demonstrative. Rigidity is the primitive; description is derived. The traditional picture — names as abbreviated descriptions, rigidity as the special property needing explanation — has it backwards. Demonstrative tracking ($x^o$) is what needs no explanation: you point at a thing and hold it. Descriptions are what need explaining: they approximate tracking only by presupposing it.


# From de re modality

De dicto: $□$(the number of planets > 7). The description stays inside $□$, re-evaluated world by world. False—there could have been fewer planets.

De re: the number of planets (that number, 8) is such that $□$(it > 7). The referent is fixed first, then the modal property is evaluated. True—8 is necessarily greater than 7.

The fixing step _is_ local rigidity. De re predication requires isolating a particular before modal evaluation. That is $x^o$: "that thing, the one I have in view." De dicto keeps the description active ($x^c$ mode), sweeping across worlds. De re discharges the description and holds the object.

Rigid designators force de re readings because they have no descriptive content to interact with $□$. There is no scope ambiguity for proper names—nothing to scope. A name is a device that has already performed the $x^o$ pick and packaged it for transport.

# From the necessary a posteriori

"Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessary and a posteriori. In standard accounts, this combination requires a special metaphysical category—necessary truths that are somehow not knowable in advance.

The $x^o/x^c$ distinction dissolves the mystery. At the $x^o$ level, the identity is trivial: same object, pointed at. At the $x^c$ level, the identity is informative: two intensional profiles—different observational histories, different inferential roles—discovered to converge. The necessity belongs to $x^o$ (same target, could not have been different). The a posteriority belongs to $x^c$ (the convergence was an empirical finding).

No special metaphysical category needed. The necessary a posteriori is what you get whenever two $x^c$ profiles turn out to share an $x^o$ anchor. The phenomenon is a direct consequence of words having two functional modes.

# Baptismal lifting as ordinary social event

Consider a concrete sequence:

"This man in front of me..." — pure $x^o$. The common noun "man" functions as a local name. R(mano,this particular). No proper name is needed because the man is present. Demonstrative contact is doing all the referential work.

"By the way, what's your name? — John." — the name is received. At this moment the $x^o$ grip is already in place. The name does not create the reference; it attaches to a coordination that is already running. R(ho,Johnc) forms \*through\* the existing demonstrative contact.

"Well, John here..." — both modes operate simultaneously. "John" does $N^c$ work: it is a name, socially portable, usable after the man leaves. "Here" still does $x^o$ work: the man is present. The phrase catches the transition mid-stride: the demonstrative has not let go yet, and the name has already taken hold.

After the man leaves, "this man" stops functioning. "John" continues. The name carries the $x^o$ grip forward into absence.

This sequence illustrates what Kripke's "initial baptism" describes abstractly: baptism is not a founding act that creates reference from nothing, but a transfer that attaches a portable device ($N^c$) to a demonstrative coordination ($x^o$) already in progress. Kripke's own illustration — "a baby is born; his parents call him by a certain name" (*Naming and Necessity*, p. 91) — presupposes the demonstrative contact without making it visible. The literature on baptism and causal chains (Kripke 1980, Evans 1982, Kaplan 1989) treats the naming event as the starting point of reference. In the sequence above, the naming event is a *secondary* act: $x^o$ was there first, and the name latches onto it.


# The convergence

In each case — the formula, Russell, descriptions, de re modality, the necessary a posteriori — the analysis arrives at the same point: something has to hold a particular fixed, and that something is not a description, not a name's semantic content, and not the formal apparatus. It is demonstrative contact: $x^o$. The formula gets it from the assignment function. Russell saw it in acquaintance. Descriptions borrow it through scope. De re predication requires it before modal evaluation can begin. The necessary a posteriori is the difference between $x^o$ (where identity is trivial) and $x^c$ (where it is discoverable). PRU's $x^o/x^c$ distinction names and explains what all five point to.
