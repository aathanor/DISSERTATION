**Objective.** The semantic picture, compressed. Russell's positive role; descriptivism's modal failure; Kripke's solution; the gap PRU fills.

## 3.1 Russell and Frege: Names as Disguised Descriptions

The descriptivist thesis: a proper name abbreviates a cluster of definite descriptions that uniquely picks out an individual. Russell's deeper move within this tradition: genuine names require acquaintance; only demonstratives qualify. Russell discovers local rigidity — the referential certainty of demonstrative use under acquaintance — then retreats by treating it as a limiting case restricted to immediate sense data.

The retreat is forced by the Cartesian demand for infallibility: identity must be "exempt from all imaginable doubt." Outside immediate sense data, doubt is possible; therefore (for Russell) rigid reference is impossible. The dissertation argues, against Russell, that rigidity does not require infallibility.

## 3.2 The Modal Failure of Descriptions

Descriptions pick out satisfiers world-by-world — constitutively world-relative. No accumulation of descriptive conditions produces cross-world tracking. The scope problem makes this formally explicit: $\iota x\, Fx$ relative to $\Box$ has primary and secondary occurrences with different truth conditions. Pulling $\iota x\, Fx$ outside the modal operator (large scope) mimics rigidity but only positionally — the description is evaluated once in the actual world, its denotation fixed, then the modal operator cannot re-run the sweep.

Kaplan's *dthat* operator makes this explicit: $dthat(\iota x\, Fx)$ takes a description and rigidifies it. Large-scope $\iota x\, Fx$ and $dthat(\iota x\, Fx)$ do the same logical work. *Dthat* is nameification: converting derived rigidity into intrinsic rigidity by stipulation. The community recognized the gap within its own formal framework. Descriptions approximate rigidity only by becoming name-like; rigidity is the prior phenomenon.

## 3.3 Kripke's Rigid Designation

Core definition: a designator is rigid if it picks out the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists. *De jure* rigidity (the term is stipulated to refer to one individual) versus *de facto* rigidity (the description happens to pick out the same individual in every world).

The anti-descriptivist argument operates on three fronts:

- **Modal argument.** "Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" is intelligible. If "Aristotle" abbreviated "the teacher of Alexander," the sentence would be a contradiction.
- **Epistemic argument.** Speakers can use a name competently without knowing any uniquely identifying description of the bearer.
- **Semantic argument.** The descriptions associated with a name can be largely false without the name failing to refer.

Reference is fixed by baptism and preserved through causal chains of communication: historical contact, not descriptive content.

## 3.4 The Explanatory Gap

What the semantic account leaves open: no cognitive story of rigid tracking, no cognitive mechanism for baptism, no psychological realization of the causal chain. Russell gestured at the answer (acquaintance) but couldn't generalize beyond immediate sense data. Kaplan's *dthat* formalizes nameification but stipulates rigidity by fiat.

The post-Kripkean literature acknowledges the gap openly. LaPorte's "promissory note" formulation captures it: Kripke's apparatus tells us *that* names are rigid, not *how* speakers achieve rigid tracking.

## 3.5 Existing Cognitive Implementations and Their Limits

Three serious attempts to fill the gap, each with a shared limitation:

- **Evans: discriminating conception.** The right desideratum — a speaker must be able to discriminate the bearer from other things — misdescribed as a "conception." The word pulls the explanation back into the epistemic-propositional register.
- **Recanati: mental files.** Container metaphor — a file holds information about a bearer. Propositional, static.
- **Devitt: causal-descriptivism.** Closer than the others — keeps the causal chain as the reference-fixing mechanism — but still representationalist about what makes the chain work.

The shared limitation: all retain the template architecture in which a stored content is matched against perception. None explains the cognitive primitive from which rigid tracking emerges. The grouping of these three under one diagnosis is offered as the dissertation's interpretive claim, not as consensus.
