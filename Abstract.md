# Abstract

The dissertation argues that Kripke's account of rigid designation rests on a cognitive precondition his semantics presupposes but does not theorize. The natural-language gloss of $\forall x \forall y\,(x = y
\rightarrow \Box(x = y))$ — "for every object $x$ and object $y$" —passes through three distinct readings of "object": mathematical variables rigid by assignment, encountered instances rigid within an encounter, and named individuals rigid across absence. The formula is valid for the first. The philosophical claim concerns the third. The transition is unargued in Kripke and largely unremarked in the secondary literature.

The thesis is that rigid designation is the social stabilization of $x^o$-mode tracking across absence, where $x^o$ marks the use of a word in coordination with an experienced instance and $x^c$ marks its use within linguistic relations. The PRU framework supplies the distinction as a single token's two functional registers, and the dissertation uses it to disambiguate what the necessity-of-identity formula treats as one phenomenon: identity is trivial at $x^o$ (Wittgenstein's point in
Tractatus 5.5303) and substantive at $x^c$; necessity attaches to the $x^o$ anchor; the necessary a posteriori arises because one identity statement straddles both levels.

The argument runs through Russell, Kripke, and the post-Kripkean cognitive literature. Russell identified the mechanism — demonstrative reference under acquaintance — and then restricted it to immediate sense-data on Cartesian grounds. Kripke extended the scope of rigidity to natural-language names but inherited Russell's restraint by leaving the mechanism unspecified. Existing cognitive accounts (Evans, Recanati, Devitt) point at the right desideratum but redescribe it in representationalist terms. The dissertation locates the mechanism Russell discovered and Kripke extended in $x^o$-mode tracking, with baptismal lifting carrying that tracking across absence through social practice.
