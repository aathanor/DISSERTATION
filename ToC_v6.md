# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## The Problem Kripke Left Open

Kripke establishes what rigid designators do; he does not analyze the cognitive operation that makes such tracking possible. The gap is not semantic but metasemantic: how a word comes to hold a particular fixed across absence, counterfactual variation, and descriptive error. The gap sits in a specific place in Kripke's own account — baptism and the causal chain are invoked but not cognitively analyzed — and reflects a broader pattern in analytic philosophy of language in which formal operations are often treated as sufficient for what reference is.

## Two Operations of Fixing

The dissertation distinguishes two operations by which a linguistic element can be tied to a value. A-grounding operates through structures internal to the linguistic system: assignment, descriptive specification, logical and mathematical construction. R-grounding operates through coordination with something outside the linguistic system, specifically through a word's meeting with an experienced particular. The distinction is introduced informally here; its formal development, in PRU terms, is in Chapter 4. What matters at this point is that the dissertation treats these as two non-interchangeable operations, not as a single operation described in different vocabularies.

## Thesis: Local Rigidity Is Primitive

Rigid designation in the Kripkean sense is the social stabilization of a more primitive phenomenon: local rigidity. Local rigidity is produced by live R-grounding in demonstrative encounter, where a word coordinates with a present particular without descriptive mediation. Proper names inherit and stabilize this achievement across absence. The thesis has two parts: an account of how local rigidity is lifted into a name at baptism, and an account of what happens after the baptismal community is gone and contemporary speakers use the name without direct access to its bearer. The second part is where the dissertation goes beyond the standard picture.

##  The Four Lines of Support

Taking as its starting point the semantic consensus that Kripke's formula presupposes rigidity and descriptions fail modally, the positive thesis is supported by four converging lines. Line 1: De re predication requires the prior isolation of a particular before any modal evaluation can occur. Line 2: Russell identified the exact cognitive mechanism for this isolation under demonstrative acquaintance, but restricted it too narrowly. Line 3: Ordinary baptism is not a semantic primitive, but the observable event where naming latches onto this already active demonstrative grip to preserve it across absence. Line 4: The necessary a posteriori becomes cognitively intelligible once the distinction between object-mode and concept-mode is drawn. No single line is load-bearing; the argument depends on their convergence to close the metasemantic gap.

## Methodology and Scope

The dissertation is a work in the metasemantics of singular reference. It proceeds through close reading of Kripke, Russell, Wittgenstein, and Kaplan, using PRU as an analytical framework for distinguishing object-mode, concept-mode, and the grounding operations. The scope is proper names and singular reference. General-term rigidity, fictional names, and mathematical reference are flagged but not developed. The diagnostic application of the A-/R-grounding distinction to Russell, Quine, and Lewis is mentioned in passing but reserved for a separate paper.

This chapter develops Lines 2 and 3. Russell identified the mechanism and retreated; descriptions cannot generate rigidity from within; Kaplan's *dthat* shows this plainly; Kripke correctly names the phenomenon but leaves the cognitive operation unspecified.

Descriptions do not become rigid from within. When they appear rigid, they borrow rigidity from an already secured referential hold. The invention of *dthat* is the clearest sign that the formal tradition recognized the gap even while trying to close it from within the description side.

## 3.5 Kripke's Rigid Designation

Kripke correctly identifies the phenomenon names exhibit and correctly opposes descriptivism. What remains open is the cognitive operation by which the relation is first established and then sustained.

## 3.6 The Explanatory Gap

The gap stated precisely: Russell had the mechanism but not the generalization; Kripke had the generalization but left the mechanism at the level of an unanalyzed primitive.

# Chapter 4 — The $x^o$ / $x^c$ Distinction and the Grounding Operations

This chapter is the analytical core. It introduces the distinction between object-mode and concept-mode, develops the two grounding operations formally, shows why the grounding distinction is not a restatement of the mode distinction, and uses the full vocabulary to treat three further lines of support.

## 4.1 Two Modes of One Word

A single word can function in object-mode ($x^o$), coordinating with a present or perceptually available particular, or in concept-mode ($x^c$), operating within the inferential and relational network of language.

## 4.2 A- and R-Grounding: Formal Statement

R-grounding: $\mathcal{R}(x^o, x^c)$, where a word in object-mode is coordinated with an active experiential pattern activation $E(\{X\})$ and thereby ties a concept-word to a particular. A-grounding: $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}, x^c)$, where a concept-word is tied to a value through a $\langle x \rangle$-resident structure $\mathcal{D}$ — a description, a bound variable under an assignment function, a logical or mathematical specification, a narrative attribution. The two operations are not rivals but different operations that do different work.

## 4.3 Why the Grounding Distinction Is Not a Restatement

The mode distinction and the grounding distinction are not two names for the same thing. Memory-based reference in absence — "the chair we saw in IKEA" — shows where they come apart. The word "chair" in this use is not functioning in object-mode (no active $E(\{CHAIR\})$) but not cleanly in concept-mode either (the reference is to a particular, not to the generic network of chair-talk). What resolves this is $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}, chair^c)$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is descriptive material drawn from memory that retains a link to an earlier R-grounded encounter. This case is not cleanly describable with the mode distinction alone; the grounding distinction earns its place by handling it.

## 4.4 Identity and the Necessary A Posteriori

The distinction resolves the puzzle by showing how one and the same statement can be necessary relative to the object fixed and yet discovered only through the convergence of conceptually distinct routes.

## 4.5 De Re Modality

De re predication presupposes prior fixation of a particular. This fixation is the functional role of $x^o$ in live cases and of the categorial feature of names in absence cases.

## 4.6 Names as the $x^o$/$x^c$ Interface

Names join two strata: the object they track and the descriptive or narrative material speakers accumulate around them. This explains how a name can remain referentially stable even when the speaker's beliefs are thin or mistaken.

## 4.7 Counterfactual Reasoning and Selective Ungrounding

Counterfactual reasoning works by releasing some material to $x^c$ while holding the individual fixed through its anchored $x^o$ or through the categorial rigidity of its name. Local rigidity matters for modality because it makes selective ungrounding possible. This section preserves the strongest older insight in the dissertation's materials.

## 4.8 Baptism as an Ordinary Social Event

The ordinary exchange makes visible the sequence formalized in Chapter 5: before naming, there is already a live demonstrative coordination; naming supplies a portable linguistic device that preserves that achieved fixation into absence.

## 4.9 The Convergence

Necessary a posteriori identities, de re predication, selective ungrounding, and baptism are not separate mysteries. They are different manifestations of the same underlying distinction.

# Chapter 5 — The PRU Account of Rigid Designation

This chapter is the positive account. The argumentative spine runs from the cognitive primitive through baptism as its lifting, to the problem of absence and its resolution through A-grounding, to the role of causal chains and a brief coda on possible worlds.

## 5.1 PRU Vocabulary and $x^o$ as the Cognitive Primitive

Pattern constellations, linguistic networks, and the grounding operator are introduced only to the degree required. The fundamental referential achievement is not description but live coordination with a particular: Russell's mechanism without Russell's infallibilism. R requires an active experiential pattern; when the particular is absent, the operation is no longer available.

## 5.2 Baptism as Lifting: $\mathcal{R}(h^o, h^c) \rightarrow \mathcal{R}(h^o, N^c)$

The formal core of the chapter. Before baptism, a live demonstrative coordination is active with the ordinary concept-word. Baptism introduces a new concept-word — a proper name — whose standing function is to preserve the coordination into absence. The operation $\mathcal{R}$ does not change; what changes is the concept-word it coordinates with. The baptismal exchange from §4.8 is the observable form of this transition.

## 5.3 From R to A: The Transition Through Absence

Once the bearer is gone, R is no longer available to anyone. Contemporary speakers who use the name operate through $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}, N^c)$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is the descriptive and testimonial material the speaker has accumulated. This is not descriptivism: the descriptive material does not define the referent (the referent was fixed at baptism, through a real R-grounding with a real individual), but it does serve as the speaker's cognitive handle on a name whose rigidity is secured elsewhere. Proper names as a category of linguistic item are treated by competent speakers as tracking unique individuals who actually existed and were originally baptized; this categorial feature is what allows reference to persist after R is no longer available.

## 5.4 Causal Chains as Descriptive and Testimonial Transmission

The Kripkean chain is not a bare causal relay but the social process through which descriptive and testimonial material accumulates, is corrected, and becomes available to later users. What chains carry — the proportion of rigidity-transmission versus $\mathcal{D}$-transmission — is a question the present account refines rather than settles. The standard picture is too coarse about what later users actually possess.

## 5.5 PRU as Metasemantics

PRU explains the cognitive preconditions of successful rigid designation without revising the semantic results Kripke established. The account supplements Kripke's unanalyzed primitives with a cognitive-level analysis of what baptism does and how its result is preserved.

## 5.6 A Note on Possible Worlds

The account has a consequence for the Kripke–Lewis disagreement. Once the cognitive operation of R-grounding is made explicit, the stipulative reading of possible worlds — Kripke's own — coheres with the picture, while the Lewisian realist reading does not. A full engagement with Lewis is left for further work; the point here is that the explanatory level at which PRU operates makes the Lewisian picture structurally awkward for rigidity.

# Chapter 7 — Conclusion

## 7.1 The Argument Reconstructed

Rigid designation is the social stabilization of primitive local rigidity. Kripke's formal apparatus presupposes it. Russell glimpsed it. Descriptions cannot generate it. The $x^o$/$x^c$ distinction names it. PRU explains how baptism exports it into absence and how later speakers continue to use names without collapsing into descriptivism.

## 7.2 What PRU Explains That Other Accounts Do Not

PRU explains both the origin of rigidity and the structure of later name use without reducing names either to descriptions or to unexplained semantic primitives.
