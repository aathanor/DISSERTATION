Thank you for these comments — they are the most useful I've had, because they  hit deep (seem to be your specialty). I sort the replies into three classes: objections that miss the target of the account as intended; objections that don't refute the account but show the present text states it too strongly or too ambiguously; and objections that are correct and require explicit changes.

One clarification governs several of the replies. I keep the notion of _local rigidity_, but I distinguish it from _portable rigidity_. In an occurrent demonstrative use — “that bird could have been in a different tree” — the demonstrative fixes _that bird_ as the target while the modal variation concerns the tree, not the bird. That is local rigidity: context-bound, fallible, and dependent on an actual use. It is not yet the portable rigidity of proper names. What I should not say without qualification is that baptism transfers a ready-made Kripkean modal property from perception into the name. The better formulation is this: $x^o$-coordination supplies grounded local fixation; baptism stabilizes that fixation into a public name; the resulting name can then be treated, in Kripke’s sense, as a rigid designator.

# I. Objections that miss the target, but show where the text needs clarification

## 1. “Pure-$x^c$” speakers and proper names

> _If rigidity depends on $x^o$, how can speakers who operate solely at $x^c$ nevertheless use “Aristotle”, “Venus” rigidly?_

The objection depends on a collapse the account should not allow. A speaker who knows “Aristotle” only through books is not operating with a generic concept-word of the same kind as $bird^c$. She is operating with an inherited name, $N^c$, whose standing function is to track one individual through a testimonial and historical network. She need not herself have had $x^o$-coordination with Aristotle. What matters is that the name belongs to a practice whose anchoring fixed an individual, and whose later use is governed by the norm of preserving that reference.

So the objection does not refute the account; it identifies a place where the text can mislead. If $x^c$ is used too broadly, the reader treats all non-perceptual uses as concept-mode uses. A name used in absence is not merely a concept-word; it is a name-mode item, $N^c$, supported by descriptive and testimonial material but not reducible to it.

_Change._ Introduce the distinction earlier and explicitly: $x^c$ is the concept-mode use of a word inside an inferential pattern; $N^c$ is the name-mode use, a standing individual-tracker carried by a public name-pattern; descriptive and testimonial material mediate access to $N^c$ but do not constitute its referent. Stop saying, or qualify, that later speakers operate “solely at $x^c$”: they operate through inherited $N^c$ patterns, supported by $x^c$ material, and need not repeat the original $x^o$-coordination.

## 2. Demonstratives, indexicality, and local rigidity

> _Rigidity is different from indexicality. Demonstratives shift with context, attention, intention. They are directly referential, not rigid across counterfactual situations._

The objection is correct against demonstrative _types_, but not against the local de re point. “That bird” does not name the same bird across all contexts; its reference shifts with context, attention, and intention. But this does not eliminate local rigidity. A demonstrative _token_, once fixed in a context, can function rigidly with respect to the circumstances considered in that utterance. In “that bird could have been in a different tree”, the token fixes the bird locally and the modal predicate varies the tree. That is enough for the local de re point; it is not enough for the portability of proper names.

So: demonstrative types are context-sensitive; demonstrative tokens, once fixed, can be locally rigid in de re modal predication; proper names are portable devices for maintaining singular reference beyond the context in which any such token was fixed.

_Change._ Wherever demonstratives are described as rigid, add: demonstratives are not rigid in the way proper names are; their character is context-sensitive. The claim is only that a fixed demonstrative token can support local de re modal predication — once the demonstratum is fixed, the modal variation concerns what might have been true of that object, not which object is at issue. This preserves the “that bird” example while avoiding the claim that demonstratives are portable rigid designators.

# II. Objections that do not refute the account, but show that the present text is too strong or underspecified

## 3. What counts as tracking across absence?

> _What mechanisms constitute tracking across absence? Is it perceptual, cognitive, phenomenological, linguistic? How does tracking differ from mere attention or causal contact? Why is it necessary for reference fixation?_

The objection does not defeat the account, but it exposes a genuine underspecification. “Tracking” should not be left as a loose metaphor. Tracking across absence is not perceptual tracking of an absent object, nor mere causal contact. It is a mixed cognitive-social-linguistic capacity with four elements: an original or mediated anchoring of the name to an individual; a public name-pattern $\langle N \rangle$ through which the name is transmitted; a testimonial network $\tau(N)$ through which speakers inherit the use; and a norm of preservation, whereby later speakers intend to use the name for the same individual as those from whom they acquired it.

Tracking differs from attention because attention is occurrent and can shift without leaving a standing practice. It differs from bare causal contact because causal contact alone does not determine that a later speaker uses _this_ word with the intention of preserving _that_ reference. Tracking is norm-governed continuation of a name-use across absence. This is why it is necessary for reference fixation: a baptism can introduce a name, but without a practice that preserves the reference, the name would not remain a name for that individual. The point is not that every speaker re-fixes the referent; it is that later use must inherit and inhabit a reference-fixing event or practice.

_Change._ Add a paragraph in Chapter 5, before or at the start of §5.5, defining tracking across absence: not continued perception of the bearer, but the socially maintained continuation of a name-use whose reference was fixed elsewhere — preservation of a public name-pattern through testimonial uptake, correction, and the intention to use the name as one’s source used it. The mechanism is cognitive (speakers must acquire and use the name), linguistic (the name persists as a public pattern), and social (preservation depends on transmission and correction within a community).

## 4. “Importing rigidity backwards into perception”

> _Nothing in standard accounts of perception or attention entails cross-world reference. The account risks importing rigidity backwards into perception. Is rigidity an intrinsic feature of cognitive contact, or a semantic feature of expressions?_

I should not say that perception, attention, or motor coordination by themselves entail cross-world reference; they do not. Nor should I say that rigidity is an intrinsic property of perception. Strictly, Kripkean rigidity is a semantic profile of designators under modal evaluation. But the account does not need to locate Kripkean rigidity inside perception. What $x^o$ supplies is an occurrent, context-bound holding-fixed of a particular. In “that bird could have been in a different tree”, “that bird” fixes the bird as the target of the modal predication; the variation concerns where the bird might have been, not which bird is at issue. This is local rigidity: not a freestanding metaphysical property inside perception, but the local de re stability of an occurrent demonstrative use. I use “local rigidity” for that occurrent stability, not as a semantic property of the demonstrative type.

So the distinction is between local rigidity — the context-bound holding-fixed of an encountered particular in an occurrent demonstrative use — and portable rigidity — the socially transmissible semantic behavior of a name across absence and counterfactual evaluation. The objection is not a refutation of local rigidity; it is a warning against presenting local rigidity as if it were already the full Kripkean rigidity of proper names.

_Change._ Revise claims that imply “$x^o$ is already rigid in the same sense as a proper name” to: $x^o$ supplies local de re holding-fixed — in an occurrent demonstrative use, the target is fixed while the modal predication varies the circumstances. This local rigidity is not yet portable name-rigidity. It is the grounded singular fixation that baptism can stabilize into a public name.

## 5. The preserve-or-convert dilemma

> _Does baptism preserve an already rigid relation, or convert demonstrative contact into linguistic reference? If it preserves, rigidity is pre-linguistic. If it converts, rigidity is introduced by convention._

The dilemma shows that my use of “lifting” needs to be more precise. Baptism does not preserve a fully formed Kripkean rigidity already present in perception, nor create reference out of convention alone. What it stabilizes is an already grounded local fixation. In the ostensive case, someone uses a demonstrative or object-mode expression to hold a particular fixed in an encounter — this child, this human, this object. That occurrent use has local rigidity in the sense above. Baptism then introduces a standing public name whose function is to preserve that singular anchor beyond the original encounter.

So the answer is neither simple preservation nor simple conversion. It is a transformation of status: from context-bound grounded local fixation to socially portable name-use. The metaphysical commitment is modest. The account does not claim a modal property literally moves from perception into language. It claims that a grounded singular coordination can be stabilized into a public linguistic device, and that this device can then be treated, in Kripke’s sense, as a rigid designator.

_Change._ Keep “lifting” only if explicitly defined as a change of functional status, not transfer of a property. Replace “the rigidity of $h^o$ is lifted into $N^c$” with: the grounded local fixation achieved in $h^o$-coordination is stabilized as $N^c$; what becomes portable is not a perceptual property called rigidity, but a singular anchor the name-pattern is thereafter governed to preserve. Replace “the rigidity was already there” with: the local holding-fixed was already there; the name makes this singular fixation portable. Replace “the name inherits its modal profile from the demonstrative act” with: the name inherits its singular anchor from the demonstrative act; its Kripkean modal profile belongs to the resulting public name-use.

## 6. Descriptive and deferred baptisms

> _Kripke does not require perceptual tracking at baptism. Baptisms can be descriptive, deferred, empty, or based on misidentification: “Let ‘Neptune’ name the planet causing these perturbations”, “Let ‘Jack the Ripper’ name whoever committed these murders.”_

This is partly right and should change the text. It does not refute the ostensive core, but the account should not present ostensive baptism as the only structure of naming. Distinguish two structures: ostensive baptism, $\mathcal{R}(h^o, N^c)$, where a name is introduced by anchoring it in a live demonstrative/object-mode coordination; and attributive or descriptive baptism, $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D}, N^c)$, where a name is introduced through a description or causal role, with the expectation of a unique bearer satisfying it.

The dissertation’s positive account concerns the first structure, because that is where the continuity between demonstrative grounding and name-use is clearest. The second is real, but less central to the present argument: it fixes a name through a mediated route and may later be confirmed, corrected, or fail if no bearer exists. So descriptive baptisms are not counterexamples; they are limiting cases requiring a supplementary treatment. They show that not every successful name-use requires perceptual contact by the baptizer — but not that names are descriptions. The description may fix the reference without becoming the meaning of the name.

_Change._ Revise the treatment of Neptune and similar cases. Do not say “‘Neptune’ was not yet a proper name until perceptual contact supplied the missing $h^o$.” Say instead: descriptive or deferred baptisms have the structure $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D}, N^c)$ rather than $\mathcal{R}(h^o, N^c)$; they are genuine reference-fixing attempts whose route to a bearer is mediated by a description, causal role, or explanatory posit. The dissertation treats the ostensive case as central and leaves the full theory of attributive baptism for further work. Add: empty or mistaken baptisms show that not every reference-fixing attempt succeeds; this is not an embarrassment but a confirmation of the distinction between a successful grounded coordination, a mediated reference-fixing attempt, and a failed name-introduction.

## 7. Misidentification cases

> _Baptisms can involve misidentification: a speaker may point at one object while intending to name another, or use a description satisfied by something other than what she takes herself to be naming._

A real complication, not a refutation. It shows baptism is not a simple psychological act: reference-fixing depends on the public structure of the act — what is demonstrated, what is intended, what the community takes up, what later correction preserves. In ostensive cases the demonstratum usually has priority: if I point to this child and say “let her be called N”, the name is anchored to the child indicated, even given false beliefs about her. In descriptive cases, satisfaction of the description or causal role may have priority. In mixed cases the matter may be indeterminate or corrigible by later practice. The account does not pretend such cases are easy; its core claim is only that successful baptism requires some route by which a singular anchor is established — direct and demonstrative, or mediated and attributive.

_Change._ Add a brief limitation note: misidentification cases show baptism is not reducible to private intention or raw perceptual contact; the relevant anchoring is public and corrigible. In ordinary ostensive baptism the demonstrated object normally fixes the bearer; in descriptive baptism the satisfier of the description or causal role may do so. A full taxonomy of mixed and failed cases lies beyond the present dissertation, but the distinction between direct and mediated anchoring is enough for the present argument.

# III. Objections that are correct and require explicit changes

## 8. The claim that Kripke gives no mechanism

> _Kripke does give a minimal mechanism: baptism, causal-historical transmission, speakers intending to preserve reference. What is missing is a theory of the underlying cognitive mechanisms that implement baptism and sustain transmission._

This is correct, and my earlier formulation overstated. It is not true that Kripke gives no mechanism. He gives a causal-social picture — baptism, transmission, the intention to preserve reference — which is already a rejection of purely descriptive or purely formal accounts. The narrower and correct claim: Kripke gives the causal-social structure of reference transmission but not a cognitive account of the capacities by which speakers perform baptismal anchoring, acquire names, preserve reference through testimony, and distinguish inherited name-use from descriptive satisfaction. This is not a criticism of Kripke’s semantic theory; it is an extension of its explanatory target from semantic and causal-social structure to cognitive implementation.

_Change._ Replace formulations such as “Kripke does not describe the cognitive operation that makes rigidity possible” with: Kripke gives a causal-social picture of reference-fixing and transmission — baptism, communicative chains, the intention to preserve reference. What he does not aim to supply is a theory of the cognitive operations that implement these practices in actual speakers. The present account accepts that picture and asks what cognitive coordination it presupposes.

## 9. “Neither is a logical relation” and the causal-social point

> _When I say baptism and chains are not logical relations, Kripke would agree — he insists they are causal-social relations, which is precisely what his theory is about._

Correct. The text risks sounding as if I discovered a non-logical dimension Kripke ignored. I did not. Kripke’s account is already causal-social. The additional claim is only that a causal-social story still leaves open the cognitive implementation of the practice.

_Change._ Where the text says “Neither is a logical relation. Both are things that cognitive systems do”, expand to: Neither is a logical relation. Kripke is clear about this: baptism and chains of communication are causal-social practices. The present account accepts that and asks a further question — what cognitive operation must speakers be capable of performing for those practices to fix and preserve reference?

## 10. The relation to Kripke: extension, not correction

> _Are you saying Kripke fails to explain cognition, or that he does not aim to? Perhaps: this is not a criticism of Kripke’s theory, but a proposal to extend the explanatory target from semantic structure to cognitive implementation._

Yes — exactly the posture the dissertation should take. It should not present itself as finding an error in Kripke. The semantic thesis remains intact; the causal-historical picture remains intact. The present account asks a further question: what must be true of speakers as cognitive agents for the causal-historical picture to work?

_Change._ Add to the Introduction or §2.3: the present argument is not a criticism of Kripke’s theory of rigid designation. It accepts the Kripkean semantic result and the causal-historical picture of baptism and transmission. Its aim is to extend the explanatory target — from what rigid designators do, and how reference is socially transmitted, to the cognitive operations by which speakers anchor, inherit, and preserve singular reference in actual linguistic practice.

# IV. Summary of the revised position

Rigid designation in the Kripkean sense is a semantic property of proper names: a name designates the same individual across possible worlds in which that individual exists. The dissertation does not challenge this result. It asks how ordinary speakers can participate in a practice that gives names this profile.

The primitive phenomenon is local de re holding-fixed. In an occurrent demonstrative use — “that bird could have been in a different tree” — the demonstratum is fixed while modal variation concerns what might have been true of it. This local rigidity is context-bound, fallible, and dependent on an actual use; it is not yet the portable rigidity of names. Baptism transforms the status of such fixation: in the ostensive case, a grounded local fixation is stabilized into a public name-pattern $N^c$. Later speakers need not have $x^o$ access to the bearer; they inherit $N^c$ through testimony, correction, and the norm of preserving reference, and their descriptions mediate access to the bearer without constituting the referent.

Descriptive and deferred baptisms complicate the picture but do not refute it. They show that not all naming begins with live perceptual contact: some names are introduced through $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D}, N^c)$ rather than $\mathcal{R}(h^o, N^c)$. These are mediated reference-fixing attempts that may succeed, fail, or require correction. The dissertation acknowledges them as real while reserving their full treatment for further work.

The corrected slogan: names are not portable demonstratives in the sense of carrying a ready-made modal property out of perception. They are portable singular trackers — public devices that stabilize a grounded local fixation into a form usable across absence. Their Kripkean rigidity is the semantic profile of that stabilized name-use.

Replace “the rigidity of $h^o$ is lifted into $N^c$” with: the grounded local fixation of $h^o$ is stabilized into $N^c$.

Replace “the rigidity was already there” with: the local holding-fixed was already there; baptism makes the singular anchor portable.

Replace “the name inherits its modal profile from the demonstrative act” with: the name inherits its singular anchor from the demonstrative act; its modal profile belongs to the resulting public name-use.

Replace “descriptive baptisms are not yet proper names until later perceptual grounding occurs” with: descriptive baptisms are mediated reference-fixing attempts of the form $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D}, N^c)$; they are genuine cases, but their full treatment belongs to a supplementary account distinct from the ostensive $\mathcal{R}(h^o, N^c)$ case.
